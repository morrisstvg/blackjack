Reading the table: what stands out

The top of the ranking is no surprise. Hard 20 wins or pushes against everything, and it does best against a dealer 7 or 8, since those upcards most often finish on totals of 17 or 18 that a 20 simply beats. The strongest hand that involves an actual decision is 11 against a dealer 6: you are drawing to a one-card total that cannot bust, against the upcard that busts most often, which is why doubling there carries an expected profit of about two thirds of a bet.

The bottom of the table is dominated by hard 14, 15 and 16 against a dealer 9, 10 or Ace. These are the stiff hands: too weak to win by standing unless the dealer busts, and likely to bust if you draw. Nothing rescues them. The best available play merely loses the least, which is exactly why late surrender exists for the worst four of these spots.

Scan down the dealer column and a pattern emerges that explains most of basic strategy: the dealer's 4, 5 and 6 are weak because the dealer must draw from them and breaks roughly 40% of the time, while 9, 10 and Ace are strong because the dealer usually arrives at a made hand. Almost every stand-on-a-stiff, double and split in the game is an attack on that weakness.

Finally, some pairs sit lower in the ranking than the play suggests. A pair of 8s against a 10 has a poor win rate however you play it, yet splitting is still correct: the ranking sorts by win probability of the best single play, but the split's value comes from losing less across two hands, which the expected value column captures.

Methodology & sources: all probabilities and expected values are computed live by an exact expected-value engine (infinite-deck model, a standard close approximation of a 6-deck shoe) under the rules shown on the front page: dealer stands on soft 17, double on any two cards including after splits, split aces receive one card, no resplits, late surrender available, blackjack pays 3:2, values conditioned on the dealer not holding a natural. Results agree with standard published expected-value references for these rules, such as the Wizard of Odds infinite-deck expected value tables and Griffin's The Theory of Blackjack. Because every page queries the same engine, the numbers always agree with each other.
